Tissues

Plant and animal tissues – types, structure and functions in simple language.

1. What is a Tissue?

  • A tissue is a group of similar cells that have the same origin and work together to perform a specific function.
  • The space between cells is filled with a non-living intercellular matrix – fluid, jelly-like or hard. Its amount and composition differ from tissue to tissue.
  • Levels of organisation: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism.
  • Tissues allow division of labour – different tissues do different jobs, so a multicellular body works efficiently.
  • Unicellular organisms do not need tissues; multicellular plants and animals are made of tissues.
  • The study of tissues is called histology. The term "tissue" was introduced by the French anatomist Bichat.
A group of tissues that works together to do a bigger job forms an organ (e.g., stomach, leaf). Organs working together form an organ system (e.g., digestive system).

2. Plant Tissues

Plants do not move from place to place, so most plant tissues give support and strength. Plants also keep growing throughout their life because of meristematic tissues.

Meristematic Tissue

  • Cells divide actively by mitosis. They are small, thin-walled, with dense cytoplasm, a prominent nucleus, no intercellular spaces and few or no vacuoles.
  • They are undifferentiated – they can change into any type of permanent tissue.
  • Apical meristem: at the tips of root and shoot. It increases the length of the plant (primary growth). Some shoot apical cells become axillary buds, which grow into branches or flowers.
  • Lateral meristem (cambium): a ring between xylem and phloem. It increases the girth (thickness) of the stem and root (secondary growth). Examples: vascular cambium and cork cambium.
  • Intercalary meristem: between mature tissues, at the base of leaves and internodes, especially in grasses. It lengthens the internodes and helps the plant regrow after grazing or cutting.
  • Apical and intercalary meristems are primary meristems (they appear early and give primary growth). Lateral meristems are secondary meristems (they appear later and give secondary growth).

Simple Permanent Tissues

Permanent tissues are formed from meristematic tissue. Their cells have lost the ability to divide, have become specialised for a fixed function, have vacuoles, and may be living or dead with thin or thick walls.

  • Parenchyma is the most common plant tissue. Its cells may be tightly packed or have small intercellular spaces. It does photosynthesis, storage and secretion. When it has chloroplasts it is called chlorenchyma and does photosynthesis; when it has large air spaces (water plants) it is aerenchyma and gives buoyancy.
  • Collenchyma lies just below the epidermis of dicot stems and leaf stalks. Its corners are thickened with cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin, and it may contain chloroplasts. It gives flexible support to growing parts.
  • Sclerenchyma cells are dead and lack a protoplast. It has two kinds: fibres (long, thick-walled, in groups – jute and hemp) and sclereids (short, hard, gritty – pear, guava, nut shells, seed coats and tea leaves).

Complex Permanent Tissues

Complex permanent tissues are made of more than one type of cell that work together. Xylem and phloem are the vascular tissues of plants – they transport water, minerals and food over long distances.

Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the root to the stem and leaves. It is made of:

  • Tracheids – elongated, dead, tube-like cells with thick lignified walls; they occur in all vascular plants.
  • Vessels – long tubes of dead, thick-walled cells, wider than tracheids and joined end to end. They are found mainly in angiosperms (flowering plants).
  • Xylem parenchyma – the only living cells of xylem; store food.
  • Xylem fibres – dead, thick-walled; give extra support.
Transpiration pull: water evaporates from the leaves through stomata. This creates a negative pressure that pulls water upward through the xylem. This is the main force that moves water in tall plants.

Phloem carries food (sugars) made in the leaves to all parts of the plant, in both directions. This movement is called translocation. It is made of:

  • Sieve tubes – living tubes without a nucleus at maturity; joined end to end by sieve plates.
  • Companion cells – living cells with a nucleus that help the sieve tubes.
  • Phloem parenchyma – living; stores food and helps lateral transport of nutrients.
  • Phloem fibres – the only dead cells; give support.

Protective Tissues

  • Epidermis: the outermost single layer of cells covering leaves, stems, roots and flowers. Flat, closely packed cells with little intercellular space. It protects the plant from water loss, mechanical injury and fungal invasion.
    • In aerial parts, the epidermis secretes a waxy cuticle to reduce water loss. The cuticle is thicker in desert plants and absent in roots.
    • Stomata (pores usually guarded by two kidney-shaped guard cells) allow gas exchange and transpiration.
    • In roots, epidermal cells form root hairs to increase the surface area for absorbing water and minerals.
  • Cork: the outer protective layer of old stems and roots, formed by the secondary meristem (cork cambium). Cells are dead, compactly arranged in several layers with no intercellular spaces, and their walls contain suberin – a waxy substance that makes them impervious to water and gases. Lenticels are small pores that allow gas exchange. Bottle cork comes from the cork oak tree.

3. Animal Tissues

Animals move and have organs with different jobs, so they have four main types of tissues.

Epithelial Tissue

  • Epithelial tissue covers the body surface and lines organs and cavities – skin, mouth, blood vessels, alveoli and kidney tubules. Cells are tightly packed with almost no intercellular space and rest on a basement membrane.
  • It has no blood vessels (avascular) and its cells are replaced quickly. Its permeability controls the exchange of materials between the body, its environment and different body parts.
  • Functions: protection, absorption, secretion, filtration, diffusion and sensation.

Simple epithelium – a single layer of cells; lines body cavities, ducts and tubes.

  • Squamous: flat, thin cells; helpful where substances must pass quickly – alveoli (air sacs) and the lining of blood vessels (endothelium).
  • Cuboidal: cube-shaped; secretion and absorption – kidney tubules, ducts of glands, thyroid gland and ovary surface.
  • Columnar: tall cells, often with microvilli on the free surface; absorption and secretion – stomach, intestine, gall bladder and uterine tubes.
  • Ciliated: columnar or cuboidal cells with hair-like cilia that move mucus, dust or the egg – bronchioles and fallopian tubes.
  • Glandular: modified epithelial cells that make and release substances. Unicellular example: goblet cells of the alimentary canal. Multicellular example: salivary glands.

Cell junctions – special connections between animal cells:

  • Tight junctions prevent leakage between cells.
  • Adhering junctions cement neighbouring cells together.
  • Gap junctions connect the cytoplasm of adjoining cells and allow rapid transfer of ions and molecules.

Connective Tissue

  • Connective tissue joins, supports, binds and protects other tissues. Its cells are widely spaced in an extracellular matrix (ECM) – fluid, jelly-like, dense or hard.
  • The ECM is made and maintained by fibroblasts. It contains protein fibres – collagen (tensile strength) and elastin (elasticity) – and a gel-like ground substance (proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans and glycoproteins) that cushions cells and helps exchange of nutrients and waste.

Connective tissue proper – loose and dense connective tissue.

  • Areolar (loose): loose fibres and cells in a gel-like ground substance; under the skin and around blood vessels, nerves and organs. It holds organs in place, fills spaces, stores water and salt and helps in repair.
  • Adipose: fat cells (adipocytes) with little matrix; under the skin, around internal organs and in bone marrow. Stores energy, insulates the body and cushions organs.
  • Reticular: a network of reticular fibres that supports soft organs and helps filter blood and lymph; found in the liver, spleen, lymph nodes and bone marrow.
  • Dense regular: collagen fibres arranged in parallel; strong in one direction. Forms tendons (muscle → bone) and ligaments (bone → bone).
  • Dense irregular: collagen fibres in a random pattern; strength in many directions. Found in the dermis of the skin, capsules around organs and the periosteum (covering of bones).
  • Elastic tissue: many elastic fibres; stretches and recoils. Found in the walls of large arteries (aorta), bronchial tubes and some ligaments.

Skeletal tissue – cartilage and bone.

  • Cartilage: strong but flexible; chondrocytes sit in lacunae in a solid, pliable matrix of collagen and proteoglycans. It reduces friction and absorbs shock at joints. Most of the embryonic cartilage is replaced by bone during development.
  • Types of cartilage: hyaline – the most common; nose, trachea and ends of bones; elastic – external ear (pinna) and epiglottis; fibrocartilage – intervertebral discs and knee joint; very strong and absorbs shock.
  • Bone: hard, dense tissue with a matrix of collagen and calcium phosphate. Osteocytes lie in lacunae. It supports the body, protects organs, stores minerals and fat, helps movement and makes blood cells in the marrow.
  • Bone cells: osteoblasts form new bone (osteoid), osteocytes maintain mature bone, and osteoclasts break down bone for remodelling.
  • Compact (cortical) bone: dense outer layer. Cancellous (spongy) bone: inner honeycomb layer containing bone marrow.

Fluid connective tissue – blood and lymph; a fluid matrix transports cells, nutrients and waste.

  • Blood: plasma (water, proteins, electrolytes) + formed elements – RBCs (transport O₂ and CO₂), WBCs (immunity) and platelets (clotting).
  • Lymph: when blood passes through capillaries, water and small substances seep into the spaces between cells as interstitial fluid. Lymphatic vessels collect it and return it to the veins as lymph – a colourless fluid.
  • Lymph plasma is mainly water (about 95%) with electrolytes, proteins (albumin, globulins), nutrients, waste and cells. It has less calcium, fewer proteins and less phosphorus but more glucose than blood plasma.
  • Lymph corpuscles are mainly lymphocytes – WBCs that fight infection. T cells give cell-mediated immunity, B cells make antibodies (humoral immunity), and NK cells kill infected or cancer cells without prior exposure.
  • Functions of lymph: returns excess tissue fluid to the blood, carries WBCs for immunity, absorbs fats from the intestine and removes cellular waste.

Muscular Tissue

  • Muscular tissue is made of long, contractile cells (muscle fibres) and is of mesodermal origin. It brings about all body movements and makes up about 40–50% of an adult's body weight.
  • Muscle tissue has four special properties – excitability, contractility, extensibility and elasticity.
  • Each muscle fibre contains fine myofibrils made of contractile proteins (actin and myosin). The fibres contract (shorten) and relax (lengthen) in response to stimulation.
  • Striated (skeletal): voluntary, attached to bones by tendons; long, cylindrical, multinucleated fibres with light and dark bands; gets tired quickly.
  • Smooth (visceral): involuntary, spindle-shaped cells with pointed ends, one central nucleus, no bands; in walls of stomach, intestine, blood vessels, iris and hair follicles.
  • Cardiac: involuntary but striated; short, branched fibres joined by intercalated discs; one or two central nuclei; forms the heart wall and never gets tired.

Nervous Tissue

  • A neuron (nerve cell) is the unit of nervous tissue. It has dendrites (receive signals), a cell body (cyton) with a nucleus and Nissl's granules, and a long axon that carries the impulse to axon terminals.
  • The axon may be covered by a myelin sheath – a fatty insulating layer formed by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system. The small gaps between myelin segments are the nodes of Ranvier; the impulse jumps from node to node, which makes conduction faster.
  • Nervous tissue is found in the brain, spinal cord and nerves. It receives stimuli, processes information and controls and coordinates body activities.
  • The nerve cell is the longest cell in the human body.

Neuroglia – supporting cells that protect and support neurons. They make up more than half of the volume of neural tissue.

4. Vascular and Avascular Tissues

  • Vascular tissues contain tubular structures that transport fluids and nutrients over long distances.
  • Avascular tissues lack such transport tubes; they depend on diffusion and osmosis.

6. Quick Revision

  • Histology = study of tissues; the word "tissue" was given by Bichat.
  • Cambium = lateral meristem = increases girth. Apical = length. Intercalary = grasses.
  • Tendon = muscle → bone; Ligament = bone → bone.
  • Blood and lymph are fluid connective tissues; bone and cartilage are skeletal connective tissues.
  • Only xylem parenchyma is living in xylem; only phloem fibre is dead in phloem.
  • Cardiac muscle is striated but involuntary – a favourite exam point.
  • Root epidermis has root hairs; shoot epidermis has cuticle and stomata.
  • Dead supporting cells: sclerenchyma, xylem vessels/tracheids, cork.
  • Simple squamous = alveoli and blood vessels; stratified squamous = skin, mouth, oesophagus, vagina; transitional = urinary bladder.
  • Tight junction = no leakage; adhering junction = cementing; gap junction = communication.
  • Osteoblasts build bone, osteocytes maintain it, osteoclasts break it down.
  • Hyaline cartilage = nose and trachea; elastic = ear and epiglottis; fibrocartilage = intervertebral discs.
  • Lymph is colourless; it returns tissue fluid, absorbs fats and helps immunity.
  • Astrocytes = blood–brain barrier; microglia = phagocytosis; oligodendrocytes = CNS myelin; Schwann cells = PNS myelin; ependymal cells = CSF.
  • Vascular plants = ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms; avascular = mosses, liverworts, hornworts.
