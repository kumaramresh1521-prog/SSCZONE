Biology · Science

Nutrients

Biomolecules, vitamins, minerals, cholesterol, fats, metabolism and enzymes – in simple language.

1. What are Nutrients?

Nutrients are substances living organisms need for growth, energy, repair and to keep life processes running. They are of two kinds:

  • Macronutrients: needed in large amounts; give energy and support growth and structure. These are biomolecules and water.
  • Micronutrients: needed in small amounts. These are vitamins and minerals.
Water helps in absorbing nutrients and in removing waste through urine and sweat.
* Nucleic acids are biomolecules, but they are not a dietary macronutrient class. Dietary macronutrients are carbohydrates, proteins, fats and water.

2. Biomolecules

  • Biomolecules are organic compounds needed for the structure and working of living things. They are mainly made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sometimes phosphorus and sulphur.
  • Four major types: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

A. Carbohydrates

  • Made of C, H, O, usually in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1. Also called saccharides (Greek sakcharon = sugar). They give energy and store it, give structure and help cell communication.
  • Sources: grains (whole: brown rice, whole wheat bread, oatmeal; refined: white rice, white bread, white flour); fruits (fresh: apple, banana, grapes, orange; dried: raisins, dates, apricots); vegetables (starchy: potato, corn, peas; non-starchy: broccoli, carrot, spinach); dairy (milk, yoghurt, cheese); legumes (beans, lentils, peas); sugary foods and drinks (candy, soda, juice, honey, sugar).

1. Monosaccharides (simple sugars) – the most basic carbohydrates, a single sugar molecule; building blocks of all other carbohydrates; cannot be hydrolysed further. Water-soluble, sweet, quick energy.

  • Glucose: the primary energy source; in fruits, honey, vegetables.
  • Fructose (fruit sugar): the sweetest natural sugar; in fruits and honey.
  • Galactose: in milk and dairy products.
  • Ribose: structural part of RNA.
  • Glyceraldehyde: a middle compound in carbohydrate metabolism.

2. Disaccharides (double sugars) – two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond; water-soluble, sweet, energy-rich.

  • Sucrose (table sugar) = glucose + fructose
  • Lactose (milk sugar) = glucose + galactose
  • Maltose (malt sugar) = glucose + glucose

3. Polysaccharides – long chains of monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds. Soluble or insoluble; give long-term energy storage and structural support. They are non-sugars because they do not taste sweet.

  • Starch (glucose polymer) – stored energy in plants.
  • Glycogen (glucose polymer) – stored energy in animals (liver, muscles).
  • Cellulose (glucose polymer) – forms plant cell walls.
  • Chitin – supports fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons.
  • Inulin (fructose polymer) – a prebiotic; stays in the bowel and helps good bacteria grow.
Cellulose is the most abundant biopolymer on Earth, followed by chitin.

Functions of carbohydrates:

  1. Main energy source: glucose from digestion is used in cellular respiration to make ATP.
  2. Energy storage: extra glucose is stored as glycogen in liver and muscles and broken down when energy is needed.
  3. Structure: cellulose in plant cell walls; chitin in insect exoskeletons.
  4. Protein and fat sparing: carbohydrates stop proteins being burned for energy (so they can do other jobs) and prevent excess breakdown of fats.
  5. Gut health: dietary fibre helps bowel movement and keeps a healthy gut microbiome.
  6. Part of other biomolecules: ribose and deoxyribose sugars in RNA and DNA (nucleotides); glycoproteins and glycolipids for cell signalling and recognition.
Dietary fibre (roughage) is an indigestible carbohydrate. Two types: soluble (dissolves in water) and insoluble (e.g., corn). Main sources: whole grains, pulses, potatoes, fresh fruits and vegetables. It gives no nutrients but helps the body get rid of undigested food.

B. Proteins

  • Proteins are polypeptides – long chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. They are heteropolymers (different types of monomers, unlike a homopolymer which repeats one) made from 20 amino acids (e.g., alanine, cysteine, lysine).
  • Contain C, H, O, N and sometimes S. Sources: animals (meat, fish, eggs, milk, dairy) and plants (beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, soy).
  • Essential amino acids must come from food. The 9 are: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. Non-essential amino acids are made by the body (e.g., alanine, glutamine).
  • Solubility: globular proteins (enzymes, hormones, antibodies) dissolve in water; fibrous proteins (keratin, collagen) do not. Denaturation: proteins lose their working shape because of heat, pH change or chemicals.

Functions of proteins

  1. Structural support: collagen (connective tissue), keratin (hair, nails), elastin (lungs, blood vessels).
  2. Enzymes: speed up reactions – amylase (digests starch), DNA polymerase (DNA replication), pepsin (digests protein in stomach).
  3. Transport: haemoglobin (carries oxygen), albumin (carries fatty acids and hormones), ion channels.
  4. Hormones: insulin (lowers blood sugar), glucagon (raises blood sugar).
  5. Growth factors: stimulate cell growth, differentiation or multiplication – EGF (epidermal growth factor), VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor).
  6. Defence: fight germs, help healing and coping with environmental stress – antibodies (neutralise antigens), fibrin (blood clotting, wound healing), cytokines (control inflammation).
  7. Storage: casein (stores amino acids), ferritin (stores iron), ovalbumin (nutrients in egg white).
  8. Movement: actin and myosin (muscle contraction).
  9. Sensing: opsins (detect light in the eye), olfactory receptors (detect smell in the nose).
Casein gives milk its white colour; cow's milk protein is about 80% casein. Collagen is the most abundant animal protein. RuBisCO is the most abundant protein on Earth (needed for photosynthesis). Proteins are needed for growth and repair, so protein foods are called "body-building foods".

C. Lipids

Organic compounds that are hydrophobic (do not dissolve in water) but dissolve in non-polar solvents (ether, chloroform); alcohols are polar/amphiphilic. Mainly C, H, O; sometimes P, N or S.

Non-polar solvents have no separate positive and negative poles, so they dissolve non-polar things like oils and fats.

Simple lipids – esters of fatty acids and alcohols

  • Glycerides = esters of glycerol (a trihydroxy alcohol) + fatty acids:
    • Monoglycerides (1 fatty acid): used as emulsifiers (improve texture, quality and shelf life of food); act as emollients in skin products; are middle steps in digestion and absorption of fats.
    • Diglycerides (2 fatty acids): also used as emulsifiers in food.
    • Triglycerides (3 fatty acids): the most common fat in the human body. They store energy, give insulation and protection, and help absorb fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Found in animal and vegetable fats. Fats are solid at room temperature (butter); oils are liquid (olive oil).
  • Waxes = esters of long-chain fatty acids and alcohols (not glycerol). Waterproof plants and animals. E.g., beeswax (honeycomb), lanolin (sheep wool), cutin (plant leaves and fruits).
Emulsifiers keep two liquids that don't mix (oil and water) together by lowering surface tension between them. Emollients soften and hydrate skin by forming a barrier that stops water loss.
Saturated fatty acids have higher melting points than unsaturated ones because their molecules pack tightly. Carbohydrates and fats are "energy-giving foods". Fats give more energy than carbohydrates because they have more carbon and hydrogen and less oxygen; they need more oxygen to burn, so they are metabolised slowly and release energy over a longer time.

Complex lipids – have extra groups (phosphate, carbohydrate, protein)

  • Phospholipids: glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group; major part of cell membranes. E.g., lecithin (phosphatidylcholine).
  • Glycolipids: fatty acids + glycerol or sphingosine + carbohydrate; in cell membranes, especially in the nervous system. E.g., cerebrosides, gangliosides.
  • Lipoproteins: lipids + proteins; carry fat around the body.

Derived lipids – made by hydrolysis of simple and complex lipids

  • Steroids – four fused carbon rings.
    • Cholesterol: keeps cell membrane fluid; starting material for steroid hormones, vitamin D and bile salts. It is the most common steroid, made mainly in the liver.
    • Sex hormones (from testes and ovaries): androgens (testosterone – male traits, muscle growth); estrogens (estradiol – female reproductive functions); progestogens (progesterone – maintains pregnancy and menstrual cycle).
    • Corticosteroids (from adrenal cortex): glucocorticoids (cortisol – inflammation and stress); mineralocorticoids (aldosterone – blood pressure by balancing sodium and potassium).
    • Vitamin D: fat-soluble; needed for calcium absorption and bone health; made in the skin in sunlight.
  • Fatty acids: long hydrocarbon chains with a terminal carboxyl group. Three saturation types: saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated; trans fats are a geometric form (explained in section 7).
  • Glycerols: simple alcohol forming the backbone of triglycerides and phospholipids.
  • Eicosanoids: signalling molecules from 20-carbon fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid); affect smooth muscle contraction (lungs, uterus). Prostaglandins – inflammation, pain, fever. Thromboxanes – blood clotting and blood pressure. Leukotrienes – immune response in allergy and inflammation.
  • Terpenes: simple hydrocarbons made of isoprene units; terpenoids are modified terpenes (found in fish liver oil, wool wax, egg yolk). In plants: growth hormones (gibberellins), pigments (carotenoids), essential oils (limonene).

Functions of lipids

  1. Energy storage: higher calorific value than carbohydrates or proteins (calorific value = heat released when a unit mass of food/fuel burns completely).
  2. Structure of cell membranes: phospholipids give a semi-permeable barrier; cholesterol keeps it fluid and stable.
  3. Thermal insulation: adipose tissue (body fat) keeps body heat, especially in cold climates.
  4. Protection: fat cushions and protects internal organs.
  5. Hormone making: steroid hormones (testosterone, estrogen, cortisol) and eicosanoids (prostaglandins).
  6. Vitamin absorption: help absorb vitamins A, D, E, K.
  7. Waterproofing: wax stops water loss in plants (cuticle) and waterproofs feathers and fur.
  8. Energy reserve: oils in seeds (for germination) and fats in animals (for scarce times).
  9. Signalling: e.g., phosphatidylinositol.
  10. Buoyancy: help aquatic animals like whales float (low density).
  11. Nerve function: sphingolipids form the myelin sheath, which insulates nerves and speeds signals.
  12. Food satisfaction: flavour, texture and feeling of fullness.

D. Nucleic Acids

Biomolecules that store, pass on and use genetic information. They are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts:

  1. Nitrogenous base: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), guanine (G), uracil (U).
  2. Pentose sugar: deoxyribose or ribose.
  3. Phosphate group: forms the backbone of the chain.
  • DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid): double-stranded helix that stores genetic information. Bases: A, T, C, G. Sugar: deoxyribose.
  • RNA (ribonucleic acid): mostly single-stranded; helps in protein synthesis and gene expression. Sugar: ribose. Bases: A, C, G and U (uracil) instead of T.
  • Types of RNA: mRNA (messenger) – carries genetic information from DNA to ribosomes. tRNA (transfer) – brings amino acids to ribosomes. rRNA (ribosomal) – part of the ribosome, the protein-making machine.

Functions: (1) store genetic information – DNA; (2) pass it on – DNA replication in cell division; (3) protein synthesis – DNA gives instructions, RNA carries them out; (4) regulate gene expression – small interfering RNA (siRNA) and micro-RNA (miRNA); (5) catalysis – some RNA called ribozymes act as enzymes.

3. Vitamins

  • Organic compounds needed for normal metabolism. Most vitamins must come from the diet, but vitamin D is made in the skin and vitamin K by gut bacteria. There are 13 recognised vitamins, in two groups:
  • Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K): absorbed with fats, stored in adipose tissue and liver; excess can build up and become toxic.
  • Water-soluble (C and B-complex): must dissolve in water to be absorbed; generally not stored in large amounts (B12 is an exception, stored in the liver); extra is excreted in urine, so they must be taken regularly.

4. Minerals

Inorganic elements in soil and water, taken up by plants or eaten by animals. Two groups by need:

  • Major minerals (macrominerals): needed in larger amounts (> 100 mg/day): calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride, sulphur.
  • Trace minerals (microminerals): needed in tiny amounts (1–100 mg/day): iron, manganese, copper, iodine, zinc, cobalt, fluoride, selenium (and chromium in the table).

5. Deficiency Diseases and Balanced Diet

Deficiency diseases occur when the body lacks essential nutrients for a long time. A balanced diet gives all essential nutrients in the right amounts to keep good health, support growth and prevent deficiency diseases.

6. Good and Bad Cholesterol

Lipoproteins are lipids + proteins. Their main job is to carry triglycerides and cholesterol in the blood, because these are insoluble in water. Five types:

  1. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) – "good cholesterol": takes cholesterol from the blood to the liver for disposal; reduces plaque in blood vessels and lowers heart disease risk.
  2. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) – "bad cholesterol": carries cholesterol to tissues; extra builds up as plaque, raising the risk of heart attack, stroke and atherosclerosis (arteries become too narrow).
  3. VLDL (very low-density lipoprotein): another bad cholesterol; mainly carries triglycerides to tissues.
  4. IDL (intermediate-density lipoprotein): formed when VLDL loses fatty acids; removed by the liver or turned into LDL.
  5. Chylomicrons: large particles that carry triglycerides from the intestine to tissues.

7. Types of Fats

A fatty acid is an organic acid of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen found in fats and oils – the building blocks of fat.

Saturated fats (SFA)

  • No double bonds between carbons; full of hydrogen; linear, so molecules pack tightly.
  • Solid at room temperature; chemically stable and less prone to rancidity (oxidation), so used in processed foods; higher melting point.
  • Animal sources: butter, lard, cheese, cream, red meat. Plant sources: coconut oil, palm oil, cocoa butter.
  • Risk: too much raises LDL and heart disease risk.
  • E.g., butyric acid (butter), lauric acid (coconut oil), palmitic acid (palm oil, animal fat), stearic acid (cocoa butter, meat).

Unsaturated fats

  • One or more double bonds, which make "kinks" so molecules cannot pack tightly; usually liquid at room temperature.
  • Plant sources: olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado. Animal sources: fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, tuna).

Monounsaturated fats (MUFAs) – one double bond. Sources: olive oil, avocado, nuts (almonds, peanuts), seeds, macadamia, fish oil. E.g., oleic acid (olive oil, avocado), palmitoleic acid (macadamia nuts, fish oil). Benefits: lower LDL and raise HDL (less heart disease and stroke); improve insulin sensitivity (lower type 2 diabetes risk); anti-inflammatory; help weight control by making you feel full.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) – two or more double bonds. Two types:

  • Omega-3: sources – fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), fish oil, flaxseeds, chia seeds, walnuts. Types: ALA (flaxseed, chia, walnuts, soybean oil), EPA (fatty fish, fish oil), DHA (fatty fish, algae, seaweed). Benefits: lowers triglycerides (heart health; high triglycerides raise atherosclerosis risk); brain development; may support brain and eye health, but prevention of Alzheimer's or macular degeneration is not proven; reduces inflammation (arthritis); important in pregnancy and fetal development.
  • Omega-6: sources – vegetable oils (sunflower, safflower, corn, soybean), nuts, seeds, meat, poultry, eggs. Types: linoleic acid (LA) (vegetable oils), arachidonic acid (ARA) (meat, poultry, eggs). Benefits: energy, cell membrane integrity, healthy skin (moisture), brain function and inflammation control. Omega-6 is essential; inflammation depends on overall diet and the omega-3:omega-6 balance.

Trans fats

  • An unsaturated fat with at least one double bond in the trans form (hydrogen atoms on opposite sides of the chain). Found in natural and industrial sources.
  • Natural: small amounts in products of ruminant animals (dairy, meat from cattle, sheep, goat); WHO still recommends keeping total trans fat intake low.
  • Industrial: made by adding hydrogen to liquid oils to make them solid (partially hydrogenated fats); cheap and long shelf life. In margarine, vanaspati, processed and fried foods.
  • Risks: raise LDL and lower HDL (heart disease); promote inflammation, insulin resistance, obesity and chronic disease.
Based on essentiality: Essential fatty acids (EFAs) cannot be made by the body and must come from food – omega-3 and omega-6. Non-essential fatty acids can be made by the body – e.g., palmitic acid and oleic acid.

8. Metabolism

Metabolism is the constant breaking down and rebuilding of biomolecules by chemical reactions. It lets organisms grow, reproduce, keep their structure and adapt. A metabolic pathway is a series of enzyme-controlled reactions that change one molecule into another.

  • Catabolic pathways: break complex molecules into simpler ones; release energy (exothermic), often stored as ATP. E.g., glycolysis (glucose → pyruvate), cellular respiration (glucose → CO₂ + H₂O + ATP), protein catabolism (proteins → amino acids).
  • Anabolic pathways: build complex molecules from simple ones; need energy (endothermic), usually ATP. E.g., photosynthesis (light energy → chemical energy), gluconeogenesis (glucose from non-carbohydrates), protein synthesis.
  • Amphibolic pathways: work both ways depending on the cell's need. The Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle) is the classic example – catabolic role: breaks down carbohydrates, fats and proteins to make ATP; anabolic role: gives intermediates to make amino acids, fatty acids and nucleotides.

Enzymes

  • Biological catalysts that speed up cell reactions. Mostly proteins, but some RNA (ribozymes) also work as enzymes. All metabolic reactions are enzyme-catalysed.
  • Six classes: oxidoreductases (dehydrogenases), transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases.

Factors affecting enzyme activity

  1. Temperature: activity rises up to an optimum (about 37°C for human enzymes). Cold temporarily inactivates; high heat denatures and destroys them. Enzymes of thermophilic organisms work up to 80–90°C.
  2. pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH; extreme pH denatures it.
  3. Substrate concentration: activity rises only up to a point; then all active sites are full and the rate plateaus.
  4. Enzyme concentration: more enzyme = faster reaction, if enough substrate is there.
  5. Inhibitors: reduce or block enzyme activity. Competitive inhibitors look like the substrate and compete for the active site. Non-competitive inhibitors bind to another place (allosteric site) and change the enzyme's shape, making the active site less effective or inaccessible.

Co-factors

Non-protein parts needed by some enzymes. They bind to the protein part (apoenzyme) to form the active enzyme; without them it loses activity. Three types:

  • Prosthetic groups: organic compounds tightly and permanently bound to the apoenzyme.
  • Co-enzymes: organic compounds that bind temporarily during the reaction and then detach.
  • Metal ions: inorganic ions that bond to the active site and the substrate at the same time.

Practice MCQs (15)

Tap "Answer" to check.

  1. Which of these are macronutrients?
    1. Vitamins and minerals
    2. Biomolecules and water
    3. Only vitamins
    4. Only minerals
    Answer(b) Biomolecules and water
  2. The sweetest naturally occurring sugar is:
    1. Glucose
    2. Sucrose
    3. Fructose
    4. Lactose
    Answer(c) Fructose
  3. Sucrose (table sugar) is made of:
    1. Glucose + glucose
    2. Glucose + fructose
    3. Glucose + galactose
    4. Fructose + galactose
    Answer(b) Glucose + fructose
  4. The stored form of energy in animals (liver and muscles) is:
    1. Starch
    2. Cellulose
    3. Glycogen
    4. Inulin
    Answer(c) Glycogen
  5. How many essential amino acids are there?
    1. 5
    2. 9
    3. 12
    4. 20
    Answer(b) 9
  6. The most abundant protein on Earth is:
    1. Collagen
    2. Keratin
    3. RuBisCO
    4. Casein
    Answer(c) RuBisCO (collagen is the most abundant animal protein).
  7. The most common type of fat in the human body is:
    1. Phospholipid
    2. Triglyceride
    3. Wax
    4. Steroid
    Answer(b) Triglyceride
  8. Which group contains only fat-soluble vitamins?
    1. A, D, E, K
    2. B, C, D, K
    3. A, B, C, E
    4. B1, B2, B12, C
    Answer(a) A, D, E, K
  9. Deficiency of iodine causes:
    1. Rickets
    2. Scurvy
    3. Goitre
    4. Beriberi
    Answer(c) Goitre
  10. HDL is called "good cholesterol" because it:
    1. Carries cholesterol to tissues
    2. Carries cholesterol from the blood to the liver for disposal
    3. Carries triglycerides from the intestine
    4. Forms plaque in arteries
    Answer(b) It carries extra cholesterol to the liver for disposal.
  11. Industrial trans fats are made by:
    1. Removing hydrogen from solid fats
    2. Adding hydrogen to liquid oils (partial hydrogenation)
    3. Heating butter
    4. Fermenting milk
    Answer(b) Adding hydrogen to liquid oils. They raise LDL and lower HDL.
  12. Which is a good plant source of omega-3 fatty acids?
    1. Butter
    2. Palm oil
    3. Flaxseeds
    4. Coconut oil
    Answer(c) Flaxseeds (ALA)
  13. The Krebs cycle is an example of which pathway?
    1. Only catabolic
    2. Only anabolic
    3. Amphibolic
    4. Non-metabolic
    Answer(c) Amphibolic – it works in both breakdown and building.
  14. Pairs: (1) Vitamin C – Scurvy (2) Vitamin D – Rickets (3) Vitamin E – Night blindness. Correct pairs:
    1. 1 and 2 only
    2. 3 only
    3. 1, 2 and 3
    4. None
    Answer(a) 1 and 2 only
  15. Which minerals are needed for muscle contraction? (1) Calcium (2) Iron (3) Sodium
    1. 1 only
    2. 2 and 3 only
    3. 1 and 3 only
    4. 1, 2 and 3
    Answer(d) 1, 2 and 3
