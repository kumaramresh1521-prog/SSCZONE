Reproduction

Asexual and sexual reproduction, flowering plants and human reproduction – in simple language.

1. Introduction

  • Reproduction is the biological process by which an organism produces young ones (offspring) similar to itself.
  • It is not a fundamental life process, because it is not needed for the survival of an individual.
  • Why it matters: (1) Continuity of species – new generations keep the species alive. (2) Genetic diversity – sexual reproduction creates variation, which helps species adapt to changing environments.
  • Based on how many organisms are involved, reproduction is of two types: asexual (one organism) and sexual (two organisms, or gametes from the same or different individuals).

2. Asexual Reproduction

A single organism produces genetically identical offspring (clones). Common in simple organisms like bacteria, fungi and some plants.

Advantages: rapid – many offspring quickly; no mate needed; works well in stable environments because successful traits are kept.

Limitation: no genetic variation – offspring are identical to the parent, so they cannot adapt easily when conditions change.

Methods of Asexual Reproduction

A. Fission – cell division in unicellular organisms gives new individuals.

  • Binary fission: one cell divides into two identical cells, which quickly grow into adults. E.g., amoeba, paramecium, Leishmania (causes kala-azar).
  • Multiple fission: one cell divides into many daughter cells at once. E.g., Plasmodium (the malaria parasite). Amoeba also does this inside a protective cyst (encystation) when conditions are bad; the small amoebae formed inside are called pseudopodiospores.

B. Budding – a small bulb-like outgrowth (bud) forms on the parent, grows, separates and becomes a new individual. E.g., yeast, hydra.

C. Spore formation (Sporulation)

  • The organism forms spores – tiny, tough cells with a hard protective coat that survive bad conditions. When conditions improve, spores germinate into new individuals. E.g., bread mould (Rhizopus), fern.
  • Bread mould (Rhizopus) is a fungus. Its tiny "blob-on-a-stick" structures called sporangia contain spores, which grow into new individuals when they land on a moist surface.

D. Fragmentation – in simple multicellular organisms the body breaks into pieces, and each piece grows into a new individual. E.g., Spirogyra (an alga), Planaria. (Hydra reproduces mainly by budding.)

E. Asexual reproductive structures – fungi and algae reproduce through special structures: zoospores (tiny, motile spores, common in algae) and conidia (Penicillium). Some animals also use special structures: buds in hydra and gemmules in sponges.

New plants grow from roots, stems, leaves or buds. The units that do this are called vegetative propagules:

Vegetative reproduction in agriculture: plants grown this way often flower and fruit earlier than plants from seeds, and are genetically similar to the parent. Methods:

  • Cutting: a piece of stem, root or leaf is cut and planted. E.g., rose.
  • Layering: a stem is bent to the ground and covered with soil until roots form, then separated as a new plant. E.g., jasmine, grapevine.
  • Grafting: the stem of one plant (scion) is joined to the rooted stem of another (rootstock); often used in fruit trees. E.g., apple, mango.
  • Tissue culture: growing plants from cells in a nutrient medium under sterile conditions; useful for mass production. E.g., orchids, bananas.
The water hyacinth is called the "terror of Bengal". It is an invasive water plant that uses up oxygen in water bodies and kills fish. It was brought to India for its pretty flowers and leaves, and it spreads very fast by vegetative propagation, so it is hard to control.
Which statements about vegetative propagation are correct? (1) It produces a clonal population. (2) It helps in eliminating the virus. (3) It can be practised most of the year.
Answer: (c) 1 and 3 only. New plants are genetically identical to the parent (clones). It does not remove viruses – an infected parent usually passes the virus on. It can be done most of the year, depending on the method and conditions.

G. Parthenogenesis

An organism develops from an unfertilised egg – also called "virgin birth". It can be obligate (the only way the species reproduces) or facultative (only under certain conditions). E.g., rotifers, honeybees, aphids, and some lizards, fishes and birds.

H. Apomixis

Asexual seed production – seeds develop without fertilisation.

3. Sexual Reproduction

  • Male and female gametes, made by the same or different individuals, fuse to form a zygote, which grows into a new organism.
  • Advantage – genetic diversity: offspring vary, so the species can adapt better to environmental change, diseases, predators and climate change.
  • Disadvantages: (1) Slower – it needs a mate, which can delay reproduction and limit population growth. (2) Energy and resource heavy – finding mates and raising young uses energy that could be used for survival.

Life Cycles and Reproductive Phases

  • Juvenile phase: growth and development before reproductive maturity. In plants it is the vegetative phase. Its length varies a lot between species.
  • Reproductive phase: the period of sexual maturity when the organism can reproduce.
    • Plants: this phase starts when the plant begins to flower. Some flower seasonally, others all year. Some have special patterns: bamboo flowers only once in its life (after 50–100 years) and neelakuranji blooms once every 12 years.
    • Animals: body changes (morphological and physiological) prepare the animal for reproduction. Females of placental mammals go through cycles of changes in ovaries, reproductive organs and hormones – the oestrus cycle in non-primates (cow, dog) and the menstrual cycle in primates (monkeys, apes, humans).
    • Seasonal breeders: wild birds, frogs, lizards, deer, bear. Continuous breeders: humans and other primates. Domestic animals (cow, pig, chicken) can be bred all year with controlled conditions and hormones.
  • Senescence (old age): body functions decline, reproductive ability reduces, and finally the organism dies.
Hormones and environmental factors control reproductive cycles and related behaviour in plants and animals.

Stages of Sexual Reproduction

Three stages: pre-fertilisation, fertilisation and post-fertilisation.

1. Pre-fertilisation – everything before gametes fuse: mainly gametogenesis and gamete transfer.

  • Gametogenesis = formation of male and female haploid gametes.
  • Types of gametes: Isogametes look alike (some algae). Heterogametes look different (most animals and plants) – the male gamete is the sperm / antherozoid and the female gamete is the egg / ovum.
  • Sexuality: usually two individuals are involved, but self-fertilisation happens in some plants.
    • Bisexual plants (homothallic, monoecious) have both male and female parts on the same plant. E.g., cucurbits, coconut. Unisexual plants (heterothallic, dioecious) have them on separate plants. E.g., papaya, date palm. In flowering plants the unisexual male flower is staminate (bears stamens) and the female flower is pistillate (bears pistils).
    • Animals: unisexual (separate male and female individuals) e.g., cockroach. Bisexual / hermaphrodite (both organs in one individual) e.g., earthworm, sponges.
  • Cell division in gamete formation: in haploid organisms (many fungi and algae) gametes form by mitosis. In diploid organisms (most animals and plants) meiosis makes haploid gametes from diploid cells called meiocytes.
  • Gamete transfer: male and female gametes must meet. Usually the male gamete is motile and the female is stationary (except some fungi and algae, where both move). Medium of transfer:
    • Water – in algae, bryophytes and pteridophytes.
    • Pollen grains – in seed plants, they carry male gametes to the ovule through pollination.
    • Special mechanisms – in animals with separate sexes, sperm is passed directly into the female reproductive tract.

2. Fertilisation (syngamy) – fusion of male and female gametes to form a diploid zygote.

  • External fertilisation: in many water-living organisms (algae, fish, amphibians) it happens in water. Both sexes release huge numbers of gametes to improve the chance of meeting, but the young are easy prey.
  • Internal fertilisation: in land animals (reptiles, birds, mammals) it happens inside the female body. In flowering plants, the pollen tube carries male gametes to the ovule inside the ovary.

3. Post-fertilisation

  • Zygote: can develop outside (external fertilisation) or inside the body. It is the genetic link to the next generation.
  • Embryogenesis: development of the embryo from the zygote through cell division and cell differentiation to form tissues and organs.
  • Oviparous animals (birds, reptiles) lay fertilised eggs outside; birds have hard shells, while many reptiles have leathery shells. The young hatch after incubation. Viviparous animals (most mammals) – the zygote develops inside the mother and she gives birth to live young, who have a better chance of survival because of internal protection.

4. Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

Flower parts involved

  • Sepals and petals: protect the inner parts; petals may attract pollinators.
  • Stamen: male part; makes pollen grains (male gametophyte).
  • Pistil: female part at the centre: ovary (swollen base with ovules that hold egg cells), style (middle part) and stigma (top; often sticky to catch pollen).

Pollination

Transfer of pollen grains (carrying male gametes) from the anther (male part) to the stigma (female part). It is a key step for fertilisation in seed plants.

1. Self-pollination: pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. Common in bisexual, self-fertilising plants like peas. Gives low genetic variation. It still works when pollinators are rare or absent.

  • Autogamy: pollen lands on the stigma of the same flower. Common in flowers that never open fully (cleistogamous), e.g., some peas and beans.
  • Geitonogamy: pollen goes from the anther of one flower to the stigma of another flower on the same plant. E.g., pumpkin, maize.

2. Cross-pollination (xenogamy): pollen goes from a flower of one plant to the stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species. Common in dioecious (unisexual) plants like papaya. Gives high genetic variation.

Agents of Pollination

Abiotic (non-living):

  • Wind (anemophily): carries light, dry pollen. Common in plants with small, plain flowers and exposed stamens. E.g., grasses, wheat, pine.
  • Water (hydrophily): carries pollen in aquatic plants. E.g., vallisneria, seagrasses.

Biotic (living):

  • Insects (entomophily): attracted by bright colours, sweet scent and nectar; pollen sticks to their bodies. Bees, butterflies, moths, beetles, flies. E.g., sunflower, rose, orchids.
  • Birds (ornithophily): especially hummingbirds, important pollinators in many ecosystems; attracted to bright, tubular, nectar-rich flowers. E.g., silk cotton (Bombax), coral tree (Erythrina), fuchsia.
  • Bats (chiropterophily): night pollinators, attracted to large, fragrant flowers that open at night; they play a crucial role in pollinating many tropical plants. E.g., baobab, agave.
  • Animals (zoophily): small mammals and lizards sometimes pollinate while looking for nectar or pollen, especially in flowers close to the ground or on trees.

Fertilisation in Flowers

When pollen lands on the stigma it absorbs moisture and germinates, making a pollen tube that grows down the style (the tube joining stigma to ovary) towards the ovule. The tube carries the male gametes (sperm cells) and releases them when it reaches the ovule.

Double fertilisation (unique to flowering plants / angiosperms): one male gamete fuses with the egg cell to form the zygote (which becomes the embryo). The other male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei in the ovule to form the endosperm, the food for the growing embryo.

Formation of Seed and Fruit

After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed (with embryo and endosperm) and the ovary becomes the fruit.

  • Non-albuminous seeds: all endosperm is used up. E.g., pea, groundnut. Albuminous seeds: some endosperm remains. E.g., wheat, maize, barley. Perisperm: some seeds also keep part of the nucellus. E.g., black pepper, beet.
  • Fruit protects the seed and helps in dispersal. It may be fleshy (guava, mango) or dry (groundnut, mustard).
    • True fruits: develop only from the ovary.
    • False fruits: other flower parts (e.g., thalamus) also help. E.g., apple, strawberry, cashew.
    • Parthenocarpic fruits: formed without fertilisation, so seedless. E.g., banana.
  • Seed dispersal: by wind, water and animals; some fruits burst open and scatter seeds (explosive dispersal). The seed holds the future plant (embryo), which grows into a seedling in suitable conditions – this is germination.

5. Human Reproduction

The Male Reproductive System

Makes sperm and delivers them to the female for fertilisation. Sperm are tiny cells with a tail for swimming, carrying genetic material.

  • Testes: main male sex organs; produce sperm and the hormone testosterone (helps sperm production and causes body changes in boys at puberty).
  • Scrotum: a sac that holds the testes outside the body, where it is cooler – ideal for sperm production.
  • Vas deferens: long tube carrying sperm from the testes to the urethra.
  • Prostate gland and seminal vesicles: add fluids that nourish sperm and help them move.
  • Urethra: carries urine and sperm out of the body.

The Female Reproductive System

Designed to make eggs, nurture a developing baby and give birth.

  • Ovaries: produce eggs (ova) and hormones oestrogen and progesterone. A girl is born with thousands of immature eggs; from puberty one egg matures each month and is released.
  • Oviducts (Fallopian tubes): two tubes carrying eggs from the ovaries to the uterus.
  • Uterus (womb): muscular organ where the fertilised egg implants and grows into a baby. Every month its lining thickens and gets more blood supply to prepare.
  • Cervix: lower part of the uterus, joining it to the vagina.
  • Vagina: muscular tube from the cervix to the outside of the body.

Fertilisation and Pregnancy

  • Fertilisation: sperm enter through the vagina and may meet the egg in the oviduct; the fertilised egg is the zygote.
  • Implantation: the zygote travels to the uterus and attaches to its lining.
  • Development: the implanted zygote becomes an embryo, which becomes a fetus.
  • Placenta: special tissue joining the baby to the mother's uterus; gives nutrients and oxygen and removes waste.
  • Birth: after about nine months the baby is born through the vagina.
  • Hormones control the many changes in the mother's body, ensuring a healthy pregnancy and childbirth.

Menstruation

Each month an ovary releases an egg and the uterus forms a thick, spongy lining to nourish an embryo. If the egg is not fertilised, the lining is not needed; it breaks down and leaves the body as blood and mucus through the vagina. This bleeding phase is menstruation and lasts about 2 to 8 days; the whole monthly cycle is about 28 days.

An unfertilised egg lives for only about one day.
