Approximately 13.8 billion years ago, the universe emerged from an unimaginably dense and hot singularity — a state where conventional physics collapses under extreme conditions. Within fractions of a second, an exponential inflationary expansion occurred, smoothing out initial irregularities and setting the stage for cosmic structure formation. As the universe expanded, it cooled, permitting the formation of subatomic particles and, eventually, simple atomic nuclei during the epoch of nucleosynthesis. Several hundred thousand years later, the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms, an event known as recombination, thereby rendering the universe transparent to radiation. The residual glow from this era, observable today as the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, offers profound empirical support for the Big Bang model. However, the theory does not escape philosophical and scientific quandaries. It leaves unanswered the very genesis of the singularity itself and cannot yet reconcile quantum mechanics with general relativity at cosmic scales. Furthermore, anomalies within the CMB, the existence of dark matter and dark energy, and the precise rate of cosmic expansion (Hubble tension) continue to perplex scientists. In essence, while the Big Bang Theory illuminates the universe's evolution post-singularity with remarkable coherence, it simultaneously unveils deep epistemological and empirical mysteries, suggesting that humanity's understanding of cosmic origins remains tantalizingly incomplete. 9
