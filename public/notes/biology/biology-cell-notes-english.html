Cell: The Unit of Life

Cell structure, organelles, transport and cell division – in simple language.

1. What is a Cell?

  • The cell is the smallest basic unit of life. All living things are made of cells, so it is called the "building block of life".
  • A cell can do all life processes on its own: nutrition, excretion, respiration, growth, reproduction and response.
  • Robert Hooke discovered the cell and gave it the name "cell". Anton van Leeuwenhoek was the first to see and describe a live cell.

Types of organisms (by number of cells)

  • Unicellular: made of one cell. E.g., amoeba, chlamydomonas, paramecium, bacteria.
  • Multicellular: made of many cells. E.g., some fungi, plants, animals.
Even an organism with billions of cells starts life as a single cell – the fertilised egg.

Cell Theory

  • 1838: Matthias Schleiden (German botanist) saw that all plants are made of cells. Theodor Schwann (British zoologist) found that animal cells have a plasma membrane and plant cells have a cell wall. Together they proposed that plants and animals are made of cells.
  • 1855: Rudolf Virchow added that new cells come from existing cells.
  • Today's cell theory: (1) All living organisms are made of cells and cell products. (2) All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Shape and Size

  • A cell's shape and size depend on its function, not on body size. Shapes: disc-like, polygonal, columnar, cuboid, thread-like or irregular.
  • The cell membrane gives shape to plant and animal cells. The cell wall (extra layer over the membrane in plants and bacteria) gives shape and rigidity.
  • Sizes range from micrometres to a few centimetres; most cells are microscopic. Mycoplasma (a bacterium) is the smallest cell; an ostrich egg is the largest single cell.

2. Cell Organelles

Organelles are the specialised parts inside a cell. Each does a particular job needed for the cell to survive and work.

Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

The outer covering of the cell. It separates the cell's inside from the outside world.

  • Lipid bilayer: made of phospholipids – water-loving (hydrophilic) heads face outward, water-hating (hydrophobic) tails face inward. This makes the membrane selectively permeable (semi-permeable).
  • Proteins: integral proteins span the whole membrane; peripheral proteins sit on the surface.
  • Carbohydrates: attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) on the outer surface.

Functions

  • Selective permeability: lets in nutrients and oxygen; keeps out harmful substances; removes waste.
  • Protection and support: keeps the cell intact and, with the cytoskeleton, supports its shape.
  • Communication: receptor proteins detect signals such as hormones and affect growth, immunity and metabolism.
  • Transport: transport proteins move ions and molecules by passive and active transport.
  • Cell recognition: glycoproteins and glycolipids help cells recognise each other (important for immunity and tissue formation).
  • Compartmentalisation: membranes around organelles let different chemical reactions happen separately and efficiently in eukaryotic cells.
  • Homeostasis: controlling what goes in and out keeps the internal environment stable.
  • Other functions: its flexibility and fluidity allow growth, cell junctions, secretion, endocytosis, exocytosis and cell division.
Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972): lipids behave like a fluid, so proteins can move sideways within the bilayer. This is called membrane fluidity.
Cell adhesion proteins help cells stick to each other and to the extracellular matrix (ECM). This is vital for forming tissues and organs, and also helps in signalling. The ECM is a network of proteins and glycosaminoglycans outside the cell but inside a tissue; it helps in tissue formation, wound healing and cell communication.

Transport Through the Cell Membrane

A. Passive transport – no energy needed; molecules move from high to low concentration (along the concentration gradient).

  • Diffusion: molecules move from high to low concentration until evenly spread. Gases (O₂, CO₂) are exchanged this way. E.g., gas exchange in the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs). In pneumonia, alveoli fill with pus and fluid, making breathing difficult.
  • Osmosis: a special diffusion of water. Water (solvent) moves through a semi-permeable membrane from low solute to high solute concentration, to equalise both sides. Its rate and direction depend on the pressure gradient and concentration gradient. Freshwater unicellular organisms and most plant cells take in water by osmosis.
Solute = the substance that dissolves. Solvent = the liquid in which it dissolves.

Reverse Osmosis (RO)

  • A water purification method: pressure is applied to impure water to push it through a semi-permeable membrane, leaving contaminants behind.
  • Osmotic pressure = the minimum pressure needed to stop osmosis. RO uses a pressure greater than this, so water flows in the opposite direction.

Advantages of RO: removes salts, minerals, bacteria and viruses very well; removes calcium and magnesium, so it softens hard water and prevents scale.

Disadvantages of RO: removes useful minerals (sodium, calcium, magnesium, potassium); uses a lot of energy; wastes a lot of water; slow; costly to install and maintain; membranes and filters need regular replacement.

B. Active transport – needs energy (ATP); molecules move against the concentration gradient (low → high). E.g., the Na⁺/K⁺ pump pushes sodium out of the cell and potassium in.

C. Endocytosis and Exocytosis

  • Endocytosis: the cell engulfs outside material in a vesicle made from the plasma membrane (to take in nutrients, fluids). Amoeba eats this way.
  • Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside – removes waste and secretes substances like hormones and neurotransmitters.

Cell Wall

  • A rigid, non-living layer outside the cell membrane. Found in plants, bacteria, fungi, algae and archaea (not in animals). In young plant cells the primary wall can grow; as the cell matures, a secondary wall forms on the inside.
  • Made of: Plants – cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins, proteins · Algae – cellulose, galactans, mannans, minerals like calcium carbonate · Fungi – glucans, chitin, glycoproteins · Bacteria – peptidoglycan (murein) · Archaea – polysaccharides and glycoconjugates.
  • Functions: gives support, shape and rigidity; protects against mechanical stress, osmotic pressure and germs; controls direction and rate of cell growth; lets selected molecules and ions pass; helps cell-to-cell communication through plasmodesmata (channels joining neighbouring cells' cytoplasm).
  • Middle lamella: a layer mainly of calcium pectate that glues neighbouring cells together.
Hypotonic environment and turgor pressure: "Hypotonic" means the solution outside the cell has less solute than inside. Water enters by osmosis, the cell swells and pushes on the cell wall, and the wall pushes back equally – so the cell does not burst. The pressure of the cell fluid pushing the membrane against the wall is turgor pressure (hydrostatic pressure).

Benefits of turgor pressure: keeps plants firm and upright (without it they wilt); helps cells elongate and grow; helps stomata open and close.

Nucleus

  • A membrane-bound organelle of eukaryotic cells, usually round and at the centre. It holds the cell's DNA. Called the "brain of the cell" – it controls reproduction (cell division) and cell development by regulating chemical activities. Discovered by Robert Brown.
  • Nuclear envelope: double membrane with nuclear pores that control exchange between nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Nucleoplasm (nuclear matrix): contains the nucleolus (dense, spherical; makes ribosomal RNA) and chromosomes (thread-like; DNA coiled around histone proteins). DNA carries genes – the instructions for development, function and inheritance.

Cytoplasm

  • The jelly-like substance between the nucleus and cell membrane. It includes the cytosol (fluid part), organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi etc.) and other particles – but not the nucleus.
  • It maintains cell shape, moves materials inside the cell and hosts chemical reactions.
Protoplasm = cytoplasm + nucleus. Purkinje (1839) coined the term "protoplasm".

Endomembrane System

  • A network of connected organelles in eukaryotic cells whose functions are coordinated: nuclear envelope, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles and plasma membrane.
  • Not part of it: mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes (their functions are not coordinated with this system).

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

  • A network of membrane-bound tubes and sheets. Its membrane is similar to the plasma membrane.
  • Rough ER has ribosomes on its surface; smooth ER has none. Ribosomes are the sites of protein making.
  • Functions: proteins are made on rough ER; smooth ER makes lipids. Some help build the cell membrane (membrane biogenesis); others work as enzymes and hormones. The ER also gives a surface for biochemical reactions. In the liver cells of vertebrates, smooth ER detoxifies poisons and drugs.

Golgi Apparatus (Golgi Bodies / Golgi Complex)

  • First described by Camillo Golgi. A stack of flat membrane sacs (vesicles) arranged parallel to each other, called cisternae.
  • Functions: receives, modifies and packs proteins and lipids from the ER into vesicles and sends them to their destination; changes simple sugars into complex sugars; helps form lysosomes.

Lysosomes

  • Membrane-bound sacs full of digestive enzymes (made by the rough ER).
  • Functions: digest large molecules, old cell parts and foreign material (bacteria, food); remove waste; autophagy – recycle damaged organelles; in apoptosis (programmed cell death) they burst and release enzymes, so they are called "suicide bags".

Vacuoles

  • Membrane-bound storage sacs holding water, sap, amino acids, sugars, organic acids, proteins and waste. Their single membrane is the tonoplast, which pumps ions into the vacuole against the concentration gradient in plants.
  • Small in animal cells; very large in plant cells (the central vacuole can take 50–90% of cell volume).
  • Amoeba has a contractile vacuole (removes excess water and waste; osmoregulation). Protists form food vacuoles by engulfing food.
  • Functions: storage; support (cell sap gives turgidity and rigidity to plant cells); waste disposal.

Mitochondria

  • Sausage- or cylinder-shaped, with a double membrane. The outer membrane is porous. The inner membrane folds into cristae (more surface area). The inner space is filled with the matrix. Number, shape and size vary with the cell's activity.
  • They turn glucose and oxygen into ATP through cellular respiration – the "powerhouse of the cell". Site of aerobic respiration.
  • They have their own DNA and ribosomes (make some of their own proteins) and divide by fission.
ATP (adenosine triphosphate) captures energy from food and releases it for cell work – the "energy currency of the cell".

Plastids

Found in all plant cells and euglenoids. They contain pigments that give plants their colours.

  • Chloroplasts: contain chlorophyll (green) and carotenoids; trap light for photosynthesis.
  • Chromoplasts: contain fat-soluble carotenoids (carotene, xanthophylls) – yellow, orange or red colours.
  • Leucoplasts: colourless; store nutrients. Amyloplasts – starch (e.g., potato); elaioplasts – oils and fats; aleuroplasts – proteins.

More about chloroplasts: mostly in leaf mesophyll cells; shapes include lens, oval, spherical, discoid, ribbon-like. They have a double membrane (inner one less permeable). The space inside is the stroma – it holds enzymes for making carbohydrates and proteins, DNA and ribosomes. The stroma contains flat sacs called thylakoids, stacked as grana and joined by stroma lamellae. Chlorophyll sits in the thylakoids.

Ribosomes

  • Granular structures made of RNA and protein, first seen by George Palade (1953). They make proteins. No membrane; two subunits (large and small).
  • Eukaryotic ribosomes are larger than prokaryotic ones. "S" (Svedberg unit) is the sedimentation coefficient – an indirect measure of size and density.

Cytoskeleton

A network of protein fibres in the cytoplasm. It supports the cell, keeps its shape and helps move materials inside the cell.

Cilia and Flagella

  • Hair-like extensions of the cell membrane, covered by the plasma membrane, with a core called the axoneme.
  • Cilia: short and many; move like oars to move the cell or the fluid around it. Flagella: long and few; drive cell movement.

Centrosome and Centrioles

A centrosome usually has two cylindrical centrioles. Centrioles form the basal body of cilia/flagella and the spindle fibres that make the spindle during cell division in animal cells.

5. Cell Cycle and Cell Division

The cell cycle is the series of events in which a cell copies its DNA (genome), makes new cell parts and divides into two daughter cells. It is needed for growth, development and reproduction. The two types of division are mitosis and meiosis.

Mitosis

One parent cell gives two genetically identical daughter cells. It starts with nuclear division (karyokinesis) and ends with cytoplasm division (cytokinesis). Before it, in interphase, the genetic material is copied.

  1. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible; nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear; spindle forms as centrosomes move to opposite sides.
  2. Metaphase: chromosomes line up at the cell's equator; spindle fibres attach to their centromeres (kinetochores).
  3. Anaphase: sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles and become separate chromosomes.
  4. Telophase: chromosomes unwind into chromatin; new nuclear envelopes form around each set (two nuclei); nucleolus reappears.

Then cytokinesis splits the cytoplasm into two identical daughter cells (e.g., 46 chromosomes in each human cell).

Importance of mitosis: growth and development (a fertilised egg becomes an organism); repair and replacement of worn or injured cells; asexual reproduction (e.g., amoeba); keeps genetic information the same in every cell.

Meiosis

One cell divides twice to make four haploid cells (a haploid cell has a single set of chromosomes).

Meiosis I

  1. Prophase I: chromosomes condense; homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) to form tetrads; they exchange genetic material (crossing over); nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms.
  2. Metaphase I: tetrads line up at the equator.
  3. Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes move to opposite poles; sister chromatids stay together.
  4. Telophase I: chromosomes reach the poles, nuclear envelope reforms, cytokinesis gives two haploid cells.

Meiosis II (no crossing over)

  1. Prophase II: chromosomes condense again; nuclear envelope dissolves; spindle forms.
  2. Metaphase II: chromosomes line up at the equator in each cell.
  3. Anaphase II: sister chromatids separate to opposite poles.
  4. Telophase II: nuclear envelopes form; cytokinesis gives four unique haploid cells.
Key terms: Chromatin – uncondensed DNA + protein. Homologous chromosomes – a pair, one from each parent, with the same genes at the same positions but maybe different alleles (versions of a gene). Gene – a DNA segment that codes for a trait. Crossing over – non-sister chromatids swap material, making new allele combinations.

Importance of meiosis: creates genetic variety (crossing over and independent assortment); makes gametes (sperm and egg in animals; pollen and ovules in plants); halves the chromosome number (diploid → haploid) so fertilisation restores the correct diploid number; variety helps populations adapt and evolve.
