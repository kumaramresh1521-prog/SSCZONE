Q.76: **Comprehension (Q. 76 - 80):** The menstrual cycle, frequently distilled into reductive explanations within mainstream discourse, constitutes a complex, finely-tuned hormonal symphony that underpins female reproductive viability. Typically spanning around 28 days—though significant interindividual variability persists—the cycle is anatomically and biochemically demarcated into four sequential phases: menstruation, the follicular phase, ovulation, and the luteal phase. Menstruation initiates with the systematic desquamation of the functional layer of the endometrium, an event precipitated by the precipitous decline in circulating levels of estrogen and progesterone following an unfertilized ovulatory event. Thereafter, during the follicular phase, anterior pituitary secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) catalyses the recruitment and maturation of ovarian follicles. A single dominant follicle subsequently prevails, secreting escalating quantities of estrogen to promote proliferative thickening of the endometrial lining in anticipation of potential zygote implantation. Mid-cycle, ovulation ensues, triggered by an acute surge of luteinizing hormone (LH), culminating in the release of an oocyte into the fallopian tube — a transient but critical window of maximal fertility. In the absence of fertilization, the luteal phase commences: the residual follicular structure, now termed the corpus luteum, secretes progesterone to stabilize the endometrial architecture. Without embryonic implantation, corpus luteum regression ensues, hormonal concentrations diminish sharply, and the cyclical endometrial shedding recommences. Despite the endogenous predictability of this sequence, the menstrual cycle exhibits considerable vulnerability to extrinsic perturbations — including psychosocial stressors, systemic illnesses, and environmental variables — underscoring the intricate interplay between endocrine function and broader homeostatic mechanisms.

Menstruation initiates with the systematic desquamation of the functional layer of the endometrium, an event precipitated by the precipitous decline in circulating levels of estrogen and progesterone following an unfertilized ovulatory event. Thereafter, during the follicular phase, anterior pituitary secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) catalyses the recruitment and maturation of ovarian follicles. A single dominant follicle subsequently prevails, secreting escalating quantities of estrogen to promote proliferative thickening of the endometrial lining in anticipation of potential zygote implantation. Mid-cycle, ovulation ensues, triggered by an acute surge of luteinizing hormone (LH), culminating in the release of an oocyte into the fallopian tube — a transient but critical window of maximal fertility. In the absence of fertilization, the luteal phase commences: the residual follicular structure, now termed the corpus luteum, secretes progesterone to stabilize the endometrial architecture. Without embryonic implantation, corpus luteum regression ensues, hormonal concentrations diminish sharply, and the cyclical endometrial shedding recommences. Despite the endogenous predictability of this sequence, the menstrual cycle exhibits considerable vulnerability to extrinsic perturbations — including psychosocial stressors, systemic illnesses, and environmental variables — underscoring the intricate interplay between endocrine function and broader homeostatic mechanisms.
